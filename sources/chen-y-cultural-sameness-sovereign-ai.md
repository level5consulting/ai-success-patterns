## Original post text (word for word)

What happens to cultural diversity when billions of people start thinking, learning, and solving problems through the same handful of AI models?

I had fascinating conversations with Melissa Fraser and Andrew Kirby recently on two different flights, and they left me thinking about a side of AI we don't talk about nearly enough.

Much of what we learned came from books, classrooms, families, and local teachers, in our own languages and rooted in our culture, religion, philosophy, and history.

A Moroccan family, a South Korean family, and an American family may approach the same problem very differently. But now, they're all asking the same few AI platforms for answers, shaped by what those models were trained on and what a small number of technology companies decide is appropriate.

That's why I think we need to talk about Sovereign AI differently.

Where an LLM is hosted and where the data resides matter. But what about the data it was trained on? Whose philosophy influences its reasoning? Who gets to define what a "good" or "correct" answer looks like?

Take Confucian traditions in East Asia, which often emphasize social harmony, relationships, and collective responsibility, compared with Western traditions that put more weight on individual autonomy and personal choice.

I don't think one is better than the other. We just see things differently, and I'd hate to lose that.

Even the words we use carry some of that culture. "Inshallah" (إن شاء الله) means "God willing," but changing it to "hopefully" can lose the faith behind it. Think about "long time no see" and how closely it follows the Chinese "好久不见." It doesn't follow conventional English grammar, but it became a natural part of everyday English.

What happens when AI starts correcting every sentence, polishing every thought, and recommending the most statistically appropriate response?

When every email sounds professional, every essay is perfectly structured, and every problem follows a similar reasoning framework, are we improving human intelligence or slowly standardising it?

Perhaps the greatest risk isn't that LLMs gives everyone the same answer, but that it teaches us what a good answer should look like.

And for children growing up with AI as their primary source of knowledge and guidance, will access to the world's knowledge make them more intellectually independent? Or will they gradually lose the cultural nuances, imperfections, and independent thinking that make us different?

## Notes for Pattern 22 (Claude, October 26, 2026 - for Tuesday's draft)

- **Scales the idea up:** Fabio Moioli's sameness point is about one organization (ten people, one AI, one idea). Chen takes it to culture: billions of people, a few models, one idea of what a good answer looks like.
- **The sharpest line for the pattern:** the risk isn't only that AI gives everyone the same answer - it's that it teaches everyone what a good answer should look like. That's the mechanism: the polish becomes the standard.
- **Work-level version:** when AI polishes every email and structures every proposal the same way, a team loses the voices and reasoning styles that made its perspectives different - including the ones that came from different backgrounds and cultures.
- **Solution hooks to test:** capture each person's view before anyone opens AI (ties to Pattern 05 and 08); keep the original wording where the wording carries meaning; ask the AI for perspectives from different traditions or viewpoints rather than one "best" answer; treat "it sounds professional" as not the same as "it's right."
- **Keep out:** Sovereign AI policy and hosting debates (off topic for the pattern); the children-and-education question (outside the library's audience) - possible talk or rant material instead.
