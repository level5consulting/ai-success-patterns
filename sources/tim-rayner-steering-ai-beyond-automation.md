I'm noticing a pattern. 

More and more serious thinkers are naming the limits of AI automation and making the case for augmentation instead.

This week's standout is "Steering AI Beyond Automation" by Stanford economist Gabriel Unger and Princeton computer scientist Arvind Narayanan (h/t Sandra Loughlin, PhD). 

It's worth your time. Five takeaways:

1. Automation is a choice, not destiny. Silicon Valley treats full automation as AI's natural path. The authors trace it to incentives (labour is the biggest cost, and cuts show up in this quarter's profit) and the culture of computer science, which teaches students to see the human in the loop as "a temporary inconvenience".

2. Automation is struggling on its own terms. It promises AI that is autonomous, reliable and general purpose. You can only have two. Want it reliable across many tasks? Then people stay in the loop. That's augmentation.

3. Delegation isn't collaboration. Most "human in the loop" designs leave the person "standing at the start and then waiting at the finish line." That makes humans peripheral. Real collaboration means steering the work as it happens.

4. Expertise is the missing ingredient. Coding agents work because code leaves a public record of every step. Law, medicine and most professions don't. The know-how lives in people's heads, so AI never sees how experts actually work.

5. Capability amplification is the big game. Love this line: "The most radical and interesting uses of AI are likely to be those that let us do things we could not do before." Automating what we already do produces incremental results. 

Articles like this warm my heart because they confirm the bet we made a year ago at Superesque: that automation produces diminishing returns, and human amplification has to be the way forward. We've been building on that bet ever since. This paper makes me more confident than ever that it's right.

Point 4 is the heart of it. We teach professionals to draw out their own tacit expertise - how they actually think through their best work - and build AI thinking partners that function as teammates. We give them AI agents they can continue to use to build thinking partners and other AI systems in their chatbots of choice.

Unger and Narayanan call this "decentralized technical experimentation." That means DIY innovation. Anyone can do this today. You could be amplifying your agency now, "without waiting for AI systems to be redesigned."

That's what we teach our students at Superesque. You don't have to wait for the AI labs. Go ahead and do it yourself.
