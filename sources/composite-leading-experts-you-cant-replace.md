# Leading Experts You Can't Replace: What Managers Have Always Known

*A conversation with a career operations executive on managing people whose skills exceed your own*

*Composite interview prepared for CHRO readers. The subject is a fictional manager whose experience draws on decades of established management practice.*

---

Every CHRO has watched a leader struggle to manage a team they could not personally replace. This includes the engineering director overseeing data scientists, the hospital administrator over surgeons, and the program manager over cybersecurity specialists. AI makes the problem feel new, but it is not. We spoke with **Dan Marsh**, a 35-year operations leader who has run teams of engineers, actuaries, and specialists, about what that work requires.

---

**You've spent most of your career managing people who could do things you couldn't. When did you first notice that?**

My first week running a plant engineering group. I had eleven people, and I couldn't have done the job of any of them. I asked a metallurgist to explain a failure analysis, and about two minutes in I realized I had no basis for judging whether he was right. That's when it hit me. If I can't evaluate the work, what am I evaluating?

**What was the answer?**

I evaluate the conditions and the outcomes, not the craft. I can't judge his metallurgy. I can judge whether the furnace failed again, whether he explained the risk clearly enough for the plant manager to make a decision, and whether he met his commitments. A manager in this position owns three things: clarity of the goal, quality of the environment, and accountability for results. The expertise belongs to the expert.

**Many new managers in that situation either micromanage or step away entirely. Why?**

Both come from insecurity. Micromanagers try to prove they still belong by second-guessing technical decisions, and the experts see through it right away. Absentee managers are so intimidated that they hand over everything, including the parts that are actually their job: priorities, trade-offs, and protecting the team's time. The experts don't want either one. They want someone who understands the business problem better than they do and will remove obstacles.

**How do you build credibility with people who know more than you?**

You don't build it by pretending. I learned to ask good questions instead of having good answers. "What would have to be true for this to work?" "What are you most worried about that you haven't told me?" "If you're wrong, how would we find out, and how fast?" Experts respect a manager who can find the weak points in a plan without having to solve the technical problem.

The other source of credibility is translation. My engineers couldn't get budget approved because they explained everything in engineering terms. I could take their work into the executive meeting and connect it to cost, risk, and schedule. When they saw their projects funded, they trusted me.

**How do you evaluate performance when you can't judge the work?**

I use three sources. First, outcomes: did the thing work, and did it hold up? Second, peers. Experts know who among them is excellent, so I set up structured peer review and listened closely. Third, the people downstream. If the operations team kept rejecting a design, that told me something even if I couldn't read the drawings.

What I avoided was evaluating style as a stand-in for substance. The confident presenter isn't necessarily the best engineer. Some of my strongest people were the hardest to understand in a meeting.

**What about hiring? How do you interview for a skill you don't have?**

Never alone. I brought my best technical person into every interview and gave them veto power on capability. I kept the veto on judgment, collaboration, and fit with how we worked. I'd ask candidates to explain a hard problem to me as if I were the executive sponsor. If they couldn't make me understand the stakes, they'd struggle in our environment, no matter how brilliant they were.

**How do you handle disagreement when an expert is confident and you have doubts?**

I separate the technical question from the business question. On the technical question, I defer unless peers disagree. On the business question, such as acceptable risk, cost, or timing, that's my decision, and I make it openly. I'd say, "I'm accepting your technical judgment that this has a 10 percent chance of failure. I'm deciding that 10 percent is too high for this customer." That keeps the expert's dignity intact and keeps accountability where it belongs.

The mistake is letting a business decision pass as a technical one, in either direction. Experts sometimes use technical arguments to win what are really preference debates. Managers sometimes override technical facts because the facts are inconvenient. Both destroy trust.

**What does this team need from you that they can't get anywhere else?**

Air cover, context, and growth. Air cover means protecting their focus from meetings, politics, and low-value requests. Context means telling them why the work matters and what the organization is trying to do, so they can make good decisions without me. Growth means finding the next challenge before they get bored. Highly skilled people rarely leave over money. They leave because they stop learning or feel their talent is being wasted.

**Is there a risk of becoming dependent on a single expert?**

It's one of the biggest risks, and managers usually see it too late. If one person holds critical knowledge, you have a single point of failure and a retention problem in the same package. I required documentation, pairing, and rotation. I presented it as professional responsibility, not distrust. Most experts appreciated it, because it also meant they could take a vacation.

**AI is now doing work that many managers can't do or fully understand. Does your experience apply?**

Almost all of it applies. Managing an AI capability you can't inspect looks a lot like managing a specialist you can't evaluate. You define the outcome clearly, judge results instead of method, check with independent reviewers, keep humans accountable for business decisions, and avoid depending on a single source you don't understand. The difference is that an AI won't tell you when it's unsure unless you build the process to surface that. With people, you can usually read the room. With AI, you have to design the room.

**What should CHROs take from this?**

Stop promoting managers on the assumption that they must be the best practitioner, and stop training them that way. Teach them how to frame the right questions, translate expert work into business terms, evaluate through outcomes and peer signals, separate technical calls from business calls, and protect expert talent. Those skills transfer from engineers to surgeons to AI systems. The mastery belongs to the expert. The manager's job is to make that mastery count.

---

## Key Takeaways for HR Leaders

- **Redefine the manager's role** as owning goals, environment, and accountability, not technical mastery.
- **Build evaluation systems** around outcomes, peer review, and downstream feedback.
- **Use co-interviewing** in which experts judge capability and managers judge judgment and fit.
- **Separate technical decisions from business decisions** explicitly to protect trust.
- **Reduce key-person risk** through documentation, pairing, and rotation.
- **Apply the same model to AI**: outcome definitions, independent verification, and human accountability.
