A new word to add to your vocabulary: Botshitting

bot·shit·ting (n.): the act of shipping AI-generated work you haven't verified, don't fully understand, or can't confidently stand behind.

Bad work used to look bad.  A typo in the first paragraph.  A column of numbers that doesn't add up.  Bullet points that contradict each other.  Those flaws worked like speed bumps, telling the reader to slow down and ask what else might be wrong.

The authors call these "disfluency cues," and AI erases many of them.  Polished output is where botshitting hides.

41% of the AI users surveyed say they sometimes deliver AI output they couldn't explain if asked.  Among heavy users, who spend half their time or more with AI, it's 54%.

The report opens with a scene any C-suite executive will recognize.  An analyst had AI summarize last quarter's numbers, skimmed the result and sent it to his CFO.  At Monday's business review, three figures didn't match the source spreadsheet.  The discussion derailed, and he blamed the tool.  He'd never opened the file.

A business review, sunk by botshitting.

Most organizations, the authors write, haven't replaced the old cues with anything more systematic.  They suggest a few: clear quality bars, structured reviews and explicit standards for what "good" looks like.

When the next polished deck lands on your conference room table, do you know if it was checked for botshitting?

Sources: "The Work AI Index: Botsitting, botshitting, and the hidden human labor of AI at work," Work AI Institute, 2026. Rebecca Hinds, PhD, Mark Hoffman, Stephanie Baladi, Hancheng Cao, Yong Suk Lee, Paul Leonardi, Aruna Ranganathan, Jen Rhymer, Steven Rogelberg and Bob Sutton.

The term builds on "botshit," from Timothy R. Hannigan, Ian P. McCarthy and André Spicer, "Beware of Botshit: How to Manage the Epistemic Risks of Generative Chatbots," Business Horizons, 2024.
