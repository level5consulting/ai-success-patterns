# Avoid AI Slop with an LLM Interview

**Category:** Conversational  
**Status:** Published  
**Parent Pattern:** [Begin with the End in Mind](01-begin-with-end-in-mind.md)

---

## Name
Avoid AI Slop with an LLM Interview

## Context
Any high-stakes content creation task where generic, fluent-but-hollow output is a risk: conference presentations, thought leadership articles, strategic documents, keynotes, client deliverables. Applies whenever the output needs to sound like *you* — not like AI-assisted you, but like you, with your experience, your language, and your hard-won opinions.

## Problem
When you hand AI a content task, it generates from its training data — patterns, structures, and phrasings it has seen thousands of times. The result is technically correct, well-organized, and completely indistinct. It is AI slop: content that could have been written by anyone, for anyone, about anything adjacent to your topic. It dilutes your voice, flattens your expertise, and signals to every reader who matters that no one with real knowledge was involved.

The instinct is to edit slop into shape. This rarely works. You spend more time fighting the AI's structure than building your own.

## Solution
Don't ask AI to write your content. Ask AI to interview you first.

Start with:
*"I'm preparing [content type] on [topic]. Before writing anything, interview me. Ask me 10 questions — the hardest, most specific questions you'd ask someone who claims to be an expert in this. Push me on specifics, challenge my assumptions, and ask for examples I haven't given yet."*

Answer the questions. Your answers are the raw material. Then:

*"Now draft [the content] using only what I told you in my answers. Do not add information I didn't provide. Mirror my language and level of specificity. Flag any section where you're synthesizing beyond what I said."*

The output is built from your thinking, your examples, and your voice — structured by AI, not generated by it.

## Value Consequence
Content that reads like you because it is you. Expertise that comes through because it was never replaced. An audience — clients, peers, conference rooms — that recognizes the difference between someone who used AI and someone who used AI well.

The interview also surfaces what you don't know. If you can't answer a question in the interview, you shouldn't be making that claim in the presentation.

---

## Notes
This pattern emerged from observed failure modes in professional conference presentations: AI-drafted decks that generated polished slides full of advice the presenter couldn't explain or defend in Q&A.

The pattern is a specific instance of *Begin with the End in Mind* (Pattern 1): the "interview first" step defines the content's actual source material before generation begins.

Variation: run the interview in a fresh session without providing the presentation brief first. This surfaces what you actually know versus what you think you know about the topic.
