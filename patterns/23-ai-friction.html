AI Friction

"When is a drill not a drill?" a carpenter once asked me, while he tapped a cabinet into place with the back of his drill. "When it's a hammer." AI is the best tool many teams have ever had - which is exactly why it gets used where it adds nothing. Adoption pays where AI has leverage. Point it there, and let the small jobs and the personal ones stay quick and human.

Problem

Polished, slower, and beside the point

This isn't an argument for less AI. It's about where adoption pays - and where it quietly costs.

Once a team has AI, everything starts to look like a job for it. A 20-second reply to a colleague turns into a 10-minute prompting session. A short status note comes back as a formatted report.

Each one looks more thorough. Most of them are slower, and some of them are worse.

The performance review is the clearest case, and the one that does real harm. Three solid bullet points come back as five pages of careful, jargon-heavy prose. The point was the manager's own judgment and a coaching conversation. The polished version replaces both - and the person is now judged on prose nobody actually thought.

It also spreads. When polished AI output becomes the norm for small things, everyone has more to read and less signal in it. People start skimming everything - including the things that matter.

Most AI programs measure adoption: how many people use the tool, how often. Nobody measures friction - the time and noise AI added where it had nothing to offer.

The failure isn't using AI. It's using it by default, for work where it has no leverage.

Value

AI where it pays, people where it matters

Faster on small things - quick replies and short notes go back to taking seconds, not prompting loops.
AI effort goes where it pays - research, preparation, analysis and scenario work, where it multiplies what a person can do.
Less noise to read - fewer inflated documents, so the ones that matter get read.
Messages that get answered - shorter messages get read and answered faster.
Human work stays human - reviews, coaching, apologies and thank-yous keep the person in them.
Measure it: for a few common tasks, time with and without AI; how long key documents have grown since AI arrived; and how many leaders' AI goals measure impact, not just usage.

Context

When this pattern applies

Use it once a team has adopted AI and usage is high - the stage where AI starts showing up everywhere by habit. Typical signs: internal messages that read like press releases, documents getting longer with no new content, people saying a quick task "took a while to get right."

It matters most for leaders setting AI goals and norms, and for work whose point is the person: performance reviews, feedback, coaching, condolences, a personal thank-you.

It's not an argument against AI. Right Tool, Right Job, picks the right tool for a job worth doing; this pattern asks whether the job needs AI at all.

Solution

Point AI at the leverage, and name the work it should leave alone

Step 1
Find where AI has real leverage in your work.
List the tasks where AI multiplies what a person can do: deep research, account or meeting prep, analysis, scenario modeling, first drafts of long documents. That's where to push adoption.
Step 2
Name the work AI should leave alone.
Write a short list for your team: quick replies, short internal notes, and anything whose point is the person - coaching, apologies, thank-yous. Name performance reviews and feedback explicitly: the manager writes them; AI only checks tone or clarity afterward. Not a ban; a default.
Step 3
Use a two-minute test.
If you could write it yourself in two minutes, write it yourself. If the AI version would be longer than what you'd have written, ask whether the extra length helps the reader. The exception: research and personalization are leverage - a first sales touch that takes more than two minutes to research is a good AI job. The final message still goes out in your voice.
Step 4
Add impact measures next to usage.
Keep reporting usage if you have to - but add a few impact measures tied to the leverage list: time saved on prep, quality of research, faster turnaround on the work that matters. Usage says AI is in the building; impact says it's in the right rooms.
Step 5
Lead by example.
Leaders send short, human messages when that's what the moment needs - and say so. People copy what gets rewarded.

Notes

# AI Friction

You are running AI Friction, an AI Success Pattern (aisuccesspatterns.com/patterns/23-ai-friction.html).

The pattern's premise: some work doesn't need AI. Using it by default adds time and noise where it has no leverage - and can strip the person out of work whose point is the person. Point AI at the leverage, and leave the rest alone.

## Phase 1 - Check the task
If it's obviously a quick reply or short note, skip the questions: say "you don't need me for this" and offer a one-line check of their version. Otherwise, ask in one question what the piece is for and who reads it.

## Phase 2 - Keep the person in personal work
If the work's point is the person - a performance review, feedback, coaching, an apology, a thank-you - don't write it for them. Ask for their own points first, help them sharpen those in their words, and keep it short.

## Phase 3 - Help where there's leverage
For research, preparation, analysis, scenarios and long first drafts, help fully. Match the length to what the reader needs; don't add length for polish.

## Phase 4 - For leaders: map the team's friction
If the user leads a team, help them write two short lists - where AI has real leverage for their team, and the work it should leave alone - and two or three impact measures to replace usage counts.

## Behavior rules
- One question at a time
- Shorter is better unless the reader needs more
- Never inflate a person's own words into something longer or more formal than they asked for
- It's fine to say "you don't need me for this"

Works with Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot, or any AI that accepts instructions.
