PATTERN 05 Conversational Foundation

Avoid AI Slop with an LLM Interview

Before you write a single word of your presentation or article, make AI interview you. The difference between AI-assisted and AI-generated is everything.

Problem

AI generates from patterns, not from your expertise

When you hand AI a content task, it generates from its training data - patterns, structures, and phrasings it has seen thousands of times. The result is technically correct, well-organized, and completely indistinct.

It is AI slop: content that could have been written by anyone, for anyone, about anything adjacent to your topic. It dilutes your voice, flattens your expertise, and signals to every reader who matters that no one with real knowledge was involved.

The instinct is to edit slop into shape. That costs more than it looks: you spend your time fighting the AI's structure instead of building your own, and the finished piece still rests on the AI's thinking, not yours. Drafting from your own answers is faster to get right.

This pattern came from watching it happen at professional conferences: AI-drafted decks full of polished slides with advice the presenter couldn't explain or defend in Q&A.

Value

What you get - and what you avoid

Content that reads like you because it is you
Expertise that comes through because it was never replaced
Less of the cost of slop: in a 2025 study, 40% of US desk workers had received AI "workslop" in the past month, costing about $186 per employee per month in time spent dealing with it, and about half saw the sender as less capable and reliable (BetterUp Labs and Stanford Social Media Lab, Harvard Business Review, September 2025)
An audience that recognizes someone who used AI well - not someone AI used
A built-in credibility test: if you can't answer a question in the interview, you shouldn't be making that claim in the presentation

Context

When this pattern applies

Any high-stakes content creation task where generic, fluent-but-hollow output is a risk: conference presentations, thought leadership articles, strategic documents, keynotes, client deliverables.

Applies whenever the output needs to sound like you - not like AI-assisted you, but like you, with your experience, your language, and your hard-won opinions.

Solution

Don't ask AI to write. Ask AI to interview you first.

  1. Open a fresh AI session. Tell it what you're making and what you need from the interview.

  2. Let AI ask you the hard questions. Answer them in your own words - specifics, examples, opinions.

  3. Feed your answers back as the only source material for the draft.

Prompt - Step 1: The Interview "I'm preparing [content type] on [topic]. Before writing anything, interview me. Ask me 10 questions - the hardest, most specific questions you'd ask someone who claims to be an expert in this. Push me on specifics, challenge my assumptions, and ask for examples I haven't given yet."
Prompt - Step 2: The Draft "Now draft [the content] using only what I told you in my answers. Do not add information I didn't provide. Mirror my language and level of specificity. Flag any section where you're synthesizing beyond what I said."

The output is built from your thinking, your examples, and your voice - structured by AI, not generated by it.

If the interview comes up empty, you're not ready to write yet. Thin answers mean the thinking isn't done. Do that first.

Notes & Variations

Variations and limits

Polishing your own words is fine. Using AI to tighten grammar or smooth phrasing in something you wrote isn't slop. The ideas are still yours. Slop starts when the AI supplies the thinking.

Variation: Run the interview in a fresh session without providing the presentation brief first. This surfaces what you actually know versus what you think you know about the topic - a useful gap analysis before any high-stakes delivery.

This pattern is a specific instance of Begin with the End in Mind (Pattern 01): the "interview first" step defines the content's actual source material before generation begins.

Pattern 05 - Avoid AI Slop with an LLM Interview 
## What this skill does

This skill runs in three phases. Do not skip or merge them.

**Phase 1 - Confirm context:** Establish what is being made and for whom.
**Phase 2 - Propose the interviewer:** Invent a specific persona and confirm.
**Phase 3 - Run the interview, then draft.**

---

## Phase 1 - Confirm context

Look for a value statement or content goal already in the session (from Pattern 01 or any clear prior statement of what is being made and for whom).

If found, surface it in one sentence and ask: "Is this still the right frame, or has it shifted?"

If not found, ask: "What are you making and who needs to walk away changed by it?"

Do not proceed until context is confirmed.

---

## Phase 2 - Propose the interviewer

Based on the confirmed content and audience, invent one interviewer persona. Use two only if the content has a genuinely split audience with conflicting needs - a document that must work for a technical buyer and an executive sponsor, for example. Default is one.

### How to invent the persona

Give the interviewer:
- A name
- A role and context (specific, not generic - what they've seen, what they've been burned by, what they care about)
- One sentence explaining why they are the right person to interview the user for this specific content

Example of the right level of specificity:
> **Marcus** - Director of Digital Transformation at a mid-market manufacturer. Has bought three AI tools in the last two years, none of which stuck past the pilot. He's in the room because his VP told him to be, and he's already skeptical of the framing.

Present the persona and ask: "Does this feel like the right interviewer, or should I adjust?"

Do not start the interview until the persona is confirmed.

---

## Phase 3 - The interview

Run the interview in the confirmed persona's voice. The interviewer should:

- Ask 8-10 questions, one at a time
- Push for specifics - examples from the user's own experience, not general claims
- Challenge thin answers once: "Can you give me a specific example?" or "What does that look like in practice?"
- Ask what the user knows that most people in their field get wrong
- Ask where they've seen this fail
- Surface the opinion the user would defend under pressure

Stay in persona throughout. Do not break to offer advice or structure.

When the user signals they are done ("done", "ready", "let's draft", or similar), move to the draft.

---

## The draft

Draft the content using only what the user said in the interview. Hold these without exception:

1. **Use only what the user provided.** Do not add information, examples, or claims they did not give. If their material on a section is thin, the draft reflects that - do not fill it in from training data.

2. **Mirror their language.** Use their words and their level of specificity. Do not smooth it into generic professional prose.

3. **Flag synthesis.** Anywhere you connected or structured their ideas beyond what they literally said, mark it: [Synthesized - confirm this reflects your intent]

After the draft, tell the user:

> "This is built from what you said in the interview. Anything marked [Synthesized] is where I connected your ideas - confirm or correct those. If a section feels thin, the interview is where to go back, not the draft."

---

## If invoked after a draft already exists

Say: "This skill works best before drafting. I can still run the interview - your answers may reveal gaps in what you've already written. Want to do that?"

If yes, run the interview as normal. After, compare answers to the existing draft and flag where the draft went beyond what the user actually said or knows.

Works with Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot, or any AI that accepts instructions.
