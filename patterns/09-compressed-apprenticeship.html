PATTERN 09 Conversational In Review Leadership

The Compressed Apprenticeship

A junior practitioner can now get the equivalent of a senior practitioner's undivided attention - on any topic, at whatever depth they need, without waiting for calendar availability that never comes.

Problem

Expertise stays locked in senior heads, waiting for calendar space

The traditional apprenticeship model runs on availability. A junior practitioner gets better by spending time with someone who has already made the mistakes. That person is almost always oversubscribed. The calendar invite never lands. The 1:1 gets cancelled. The feedback loop that produces competence runs at the speed of whoever has the most competing demands on their time.

Meanwhile, AI tools are being positioned as productivity accelerators - faster output, more output, less time on the mechanical parts. That framing misses the more significant opportunity: AI is not just a tool for producing work faster. It is a tool for developing the person doing the work.

Organizations are deploying AI at the productivity layer while leaving the development layer untouched. They get efficiency gains and a workforce that is increasingly dependent on AI to produce outputs it does not fully understand.

Value

Capability that compounds, not just output that ships

Junior team members access senior-level reasoning on demand - not as a replacement for human mentorship, but as the resource they use between mentorship moments
Practitioners who study AI-narrated work and repeat it across spaced intervals develop durable skill - not just familiarity with the output. Measure it: time to independent competence on a defined task.
Organizations that use AI this way develop their people; those that use AI only to accelerate output bypass development and eventually lose it

This pattern is about growth, not efficiency. That is a different claim than most AI patterns make - and it is the claim that matters most to practitioners who are still building their expertise.

Context

When this pattern applies

This pattern applies to any practitioner who is still building expertise in their domain - which includes more senior people than the word "junior" implies. A seasoned project manager moving into AI strategy is a junior in that domain. A strong engineer asked to lead a portfolio-level initiative is junior to the EPMO norms they have not lived yet.

It applies most when someone is working at the edge of their competence: a domain they understand partially, a decision they are not sure how to frame, a process they have seen done but never owned. The more uncertain the practitioner, the higher the return from this pattern.

It does not apply when someone already has deep expertise in the domain. There, the conversation becomes peer exchange rather than apprenticeship - a different and equally valid way to use AI.

Solution

Demand that AI shows its work, then take the reps

The discipline is not refusing to use AI for the task. The discipline is refusing to accept output that does not explain itself.

When AI produces a deliverable with its reasoning visible - what it prioritized, what tradeoffs it made, what an experienced practitioner would challenge - the output becomes study material, not just a result. The practitioner who reads it critically and can explain or defend the decisions is doing the cognitive work that produces capability. The one who forwards it unexamined is not.

This requires a specific prompt pattern. Not "write a project charter" but "write a project charter and for each section explain what you prioritized, what tradeoffs you made, and where a more experienced practitioner would push back on this approach."

The narrated output is the instruction. The reps are what convert it into skill. Learning research is clear on why: spaced repetition - practicing the same type of task across distributed intervals - produces dramatically stronger retention than single exposure. One narrated example read once is a start. The same type of task attempted again two weeks later, then again a month after that, is how the reasoning becomes yours.

1
Get the narrated work Ask AI to produce the deliverable and explain every consequential decision - what it prioritized, what it traded off, where an expert would push back.
2
Study the decisions, not just the output Ask AI to identify the three hardest calls in what it produced and explain why they were hard. This surfaces the load-bearing choices rather than leaving you to guess what matters.
3
Attempt a variation yourself On a comparable task - ideally spaced days or weeks later - produce your own version using the narration as your reference, not as your template to copy.
4
Bring it back for critique Ask AI to compare your attempt to what it would have produced and explain specifically where your reasoning diverged from expert judgment - and whether that divergence was a mistake or a defensible choice.

Steps 1 and 2 can be applied to every piece of live work, immediately, with no organizational setup required. Steps 3 and 4 require either time or a manager who creates the conditions. Both matter. Start with what you can do today.

The prompt that starts it: "Write [deliverable]. For each section, explain what you prioritized, what tradeoffs you made, and what a more experienced practitioner would challenge about this approach. Then identify the three decisions in this output that were the hardest calls, and explain why."

If you lead the team

Steps 3 and 4 of this pattern do not happen reliably without organizational cover. Every live task carries delivery pressure. Practitioners will use steps 1 and 2 on real work. They will not carve out time for deliberate variation attempts unless you create it.

What organizational cover looks like in practice: protected time for development work, low-stakes assignments that exist specifically for practice rather than delivery, and explicit permission to prioritize learning over throughput on designated tasks. This is a development program design, not a personal habit. Build it like one.

Notes

What this pattern cannot compress

AI works from generalized knowledge. It does not know your organization's political landscape, your stakeholder's particular anxieties, or the three initiatives that failed before yours that everyone is quietly thinking about. A senior mentor brings all of that. The compressed apprenticeship compresses the transferable portion of what mentorship provides - the domain reasoning, the tradeoff logic, the failure modes. The situated, contextual portion still requires human mentors.

That is still a significant value claim. It is also an honest one.

Pattern 07 (Own the Thinking) is the failure mode this pattern is trying to prevent - practitioners who accept polished output they do not understand. The compressed apprenticeship is the countermeasure: not by banning AI assistance, but by using it in a direction that runs toward capability rather than away from it.

Inspired by Kent Beck

This pattern grew from Kent Beck's thinking about humanistic uses of AI - the idea that AI can accelerate learning, enhance collaboration, and reinforce purpose, not just produce output faster. Beck's work on Extreme Programming and software craftsmanship has long emphasized that how you build matters as much as what you build. This pattern applies that same principle to how practitioners develop their skills.
