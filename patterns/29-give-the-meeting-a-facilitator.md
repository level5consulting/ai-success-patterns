**Lead (draft):** Every meeting comes with a pile of work nobody sees - the invite, the agenda, chasing slides, watching the clock, the notes, the follow-ups. Whoever carries it stops thinking, and often it's the leader. Give that work to an AI facilitator you've onboarded like a new coordinator: you decide what it does on its own and what it brings to you. Note taking is not enough - a facilitator helps run the meeting from the invite to the follow-up, and people keep every call. The goal is fewer, better meetings - not the same meetings with less effort - and more time for the people in them, not less.

## Problem
**Whoever runs the meeting stops thinking in it**

A project lead calls a meeting to settle three open questions. Before it, she finds a time that works for eight people, writes the invite, chases two people for their slides, and pastes the deck together at 11 PM. In the meeting she shares the agenda, watches the clock, types notes and tries to catch who agreed to do what. Somewhere in the second topic, the person from operations raises the real risk. She hears it, writes half of it down, and checks the time. After the meeting she writes up the notes, creates the action items and books the follow-up. The meeting took an hour. The work around it took three.

Every meeting carries this invisible work, and every team hands it to someone. If it goes to a junior person, the agenda drifts and the notes miss what mattered. If the leader keeps it, the leader stops leading.

Most teams think AI already fixed this, because an AI notetaker is now a click away. It didn't. **Note taking is not enough.** A transcript covers the hour in the room - not the invite, the agenda, the deck or the follow-up. It doesn't keep the meeting on its agenda or stop the clock from running out on the decision that mattered, and it doesn't check whether the "decision" in the summary is the one people actually made. A summary that sounds right and gets one decision wrong is worse than no notes - it's botcrap with everyone's name on the invite.

**And the etiquette hasn't caught up.**
- **Uninvited notetakers.** Someone can't make it, so they send their AI to sit in. The host didn't agree to be recorded, the other guests didn't either, and now a stranger's tool holds a copy of the conversation. It's rude, and it can break trust - and possibly company rules - in one click.
- **Everyone brings their own.** People send notetakers because they don't trust they'll get the notes. Six people, six bots, six slightly different versions of what was decided. When the meeting gives everyone the same pre-read before and the same checked notes after, nobody needs their own notetaker - and the host can say so in the invite.
- **A guest nobody introduced.** An AI keeping the clock or the notes is a new kind of participant. If nobody says what it is, what it records, who sees its notes and how to talk to it, people guess - and either clam up or ignore it. A facilitator gets introduced like any new person in the room.

The failure isn't using AI in meetings. It's handing it the notes and calling the meeting run - or sending it where it wasn't invited.

**Don't go overboard.** A meeting isn't only a machine for decisions. A big part of why people meet is to build relationships - the catch-up before the agenda starts, the joke that breaks the tension, the side conversation that tells you how someone is really doing. A facilitator that turns every minute into an agenda item, cuts off every tangent and times every laugh has failed, even if every decision got made. The facilitator takes the invisible work so people have more time for each other, never less.

## Value
**Leaders back in the conversation, and the work around the meeting done**

- **The leader thinks again** - the invite, agenda, clock and follow-up run alongside the meeting, so the person who called it can listen, ask and decide.
- **Fewer meetings** - the first job asks whether the meeting should happen at all, and a meeting with no decision on its agenda gets flagged before it starts.
- **Meetings that are ready to decide** - a pre-read out on time, slides in one place, and a check that what needs deciding is ready to decide.
- **Meetings that end in decisions** - time reminders per agenda item keep the last topic from getting two rushed minutes.
- **One set of notes everyone trusts** - decisions, owners and open questions are checked by a person before they go out, and everyone gets the same copy, so nobody sends their own notetaker.
- **A record that carries forward** - checked decisions and open items feed the project record and the next agenda.
- **Clear limits** - your facilitator's briefing says what it does on its own and what it brings to a person first, so nobody is surprised by what it sent.
- **Value realized from pattern usage** - Insert your own savings numbers here. Count the hours spent on invites, deck chasing, write-ups and follow-ups each week, and the follow-up meetings called only because the first one didn't decide anything, at your own pay rates. For example, arranging meetings and keeping calendars is work an administrative assistant does - the nearest match, secretaries and administrative assistants, is a role that pays a median of about $48,310 a year, and an executive administrative assistant about $76,590 (Bureau of Labor Statistics, May 2025, pay only, not benefits). Those are examples. It is a hire you did not have to make, a contractor you did not need. It lets you run with the lean team you already have. The conversation, the calls and the relationships stay with people.
- *Measure it:* hours of meeting prep and follow-up per week, **and total meeting hours** - if prep hours fall and meeting hours rise, the pattern has failed. Also: how many meetings end with decisions and named owners, and how often the notes need correcting after they go out. And one count that should stay at zero: notetakers in a meeting the host didn't agree to. One check that should not move: ask people whether meetings still feel like time with their colleagues. If the answer drops, dial the facilitator back.

## When to use it

Use it for meetings that are meant to decide or move work forward: project status, planning, steering committees, design reviews, client working sessions - especially recurring ones, where one onboarding pays off every week. It fits best when the person who called the meeting is also the one who needs to think hardest in it.

Use a tool your organization has approved for meeting content. Facilitator in Microsoft Teams is one example; other meeting tools offer AI notes, and an approved AI can work from your agenda, your record and a transcript recorded with permission. What it can do on its own depends on what your tool can reach - your calendar, your tracker, your files - and what your organization and your license allow it to touch.

It is not for every meeting. Skip the AI, and the recording, when the conversation is sensitive - performance and HR matters, legal advice, a hard conversation between two people - or when anyone in the room says no. A quick check-in that ends in one sentence doesn't need a facilitator either.

**What stays with people:**
- Running the conversation - drawing people out, pushing on the risk, making the call. Making sure everyone contributes is the chair's job.
- The decisions, and deciding whose point carries weight.
- The social side of the meeting - the catch-up before it starts, the tangent that matters to someone, the time to just talk. Protect it.
- Relationships - who gets a heads-up before a decision lands, who hears bad news in person, which nudges go to whom, and the quick word with the leader before the meeting about who's upset and what she's walking into.
- Checking the notes and decisions before they go out.
- Asking people if they're okay being recorded.

## Solution
**Onboard your facilitator, set its limits, hand it the briefing, keep the calls**

This is a string: **Onboard, Don't Program -> Give the Meeting a Facilitator.** You don't program a facilitator with a list of commands. You onboard it the way you'd onboard a new meeting coordinator - who you are, what the meeting is for, what it may do, and what good looks like.

**Step 1: Name the meeting, then pick its jobs from the menu.** One briefing per meeting: your weekly planning meeting gets one, your monthly steering committee gets its own. Say which meeting this is and what it's for, then start from the menu below. Keep what fits, cut what doesn't, add your own. If you're not sure, start with three: **build the agenda from the record, time reminders, draft notes for a person to check.** Add more once you trust it.

**Leave room for people.** Before you add a job, ask whether it takes work off someone or squeezes time out of the conversation. Keep the first few minutes for catching up - no clock, no agenda, no notes. More jobs is not a better facilitator.

| Before the meeting | During the meeting | After the meeting |
|---|---|---|
| **Should this meeting exist?** Could it be a written update, one person's decision, or a shorter meeting with fewer people? | Run the clock - a time reminder for each agenda item | Draft the notes and decision list for a person to check |
| Flag a meeting with no decision on its agenda | Keep a parking lot for off-topic points | Send everyone the same checked notes |
| Build the agenda from the project record's open items ("Ask for the agenda") | Flag when the talk drifts off the agenda | Create the action items in your tracker |
| Draft the invite - a time that works, the right people, the ask in one line, the pre-read attached | Capture decisions and owners as they happen | Nudge owners before their due dates |
| Check the people needed for the decision accepted; reschedule when the key person declines | If the chair asks, a private note on who hasn't spoken - never out loud, never in the notes | Update the decision log and risk log |
| Coordinate the slide deck - assign slides to owners, nudge late ones, merge, check the deck tells one story | Quick lookups for questions in the room | Carry open items into the next agenda |
| Send the pre-read (format below), with the chair's own note | | Schedule the follow-up; ask one question - did we decide what we came to decide? |
| Confirm the room or the link works; a reminder the day before | Hold the clock for the first few minutes - time to catch up is part of the meeting | |

**Step 2: Run Onboard, Don't Program to build its briefing.** Give your facilitator the five parts, answered for this meeting:
- **Who you are** - your role, and this meeting.
- **What you're working on** - the project or series, its goal, and where the record lives.
- **Constraints** - your tools, your organization's rules on recording, data and how long notes are kept, who may see the notes.
- **Preferences** - how you want reminders, how short the pre-read and notes should be, the words your team uses.
- **What good looks like** - a meeting that decided what it came to decide, owners who know their next step, notes nobody has to correct.

Then ask it to play the briefing back. The playback is where you catch the gap - a job you assumed it would do, or one it thinks it can do that it can't reach. A common one: "a reminder the day before" lands at three different times for people in three time zones.

Save the briefing where it sticks - a saved project, custom instructions, or a pinned document your tool reads every time - so each meeting you only add that meeting's goal and topics.

**Step 3: Set what it may do on its own - and what needs a person.** For every job you picked, write one of two words: **alone** or **ask**. "Alone" can never be more than your organization and your license allow - check what the tool can actually reach before marking anything alone. Reminders, the parking lot and draft notes can usually run alone. Anything that goes out under your name or changes someone's work - sending the invite, sending notes, assigning action items, moving a due date - starts as **ask**. Take the nudge to a late slide owner: is it from the facilitator, under its own name, or from your account? From your account only after you've seen it. Anything that records people starts with the host's permission. You can move a job from ask to alone once it has earned it - and a briefing with "alone" jobs that send anything gets a second look from the meeting owner or whoever governs AI use in your organization. Someone who copies your briefing doesn't inherit your alone list; they decide their own.

**Step 4: Send the pre-read, and ask before anything records.** The facilitator sends one pre-read ahead of every meeting, in the same format each time (below). If it's your meeting, the pre-read and the start of the meeting both say an AI is helping run it, which tool, what it records, who sees the notes - and that everyone gets the same checked notes, so there's no need to bring their own notetaker. If anyone objects, turn it off. If it's someone else's meeting, ask the host before bringing any AI notetaker, and never send your notetaker in your place without the host's permission. If you can't attend, ask the host for the notes instead. If someone else's notetaker shows up uninvited in your meeting, it's fine to remove it and ask its owner to join in person or ask for the notes.

**Step 5: Introduce the facilitator, then lead the conversation.** At the start, introduce it like any new person in the room: "Our AI facilitator is keeping the agenda, the time and the notes. It records [what], and only [who] sees the notes. You can ask it for the time left, to park a topic, or to read back what we've decided. I'll check the decisions before anything goes out. If you'd rather it not record, say so now." Then lead the conversation, not the mechanics: draw out the quiet person, push on the risk someone just raised, make the call. Don't let the AI decide whose point matters (Own the Thinking).

**Step 6: Close on decisions, check them, then feed the record.** In the last five minutes, have the facilitator read back decisions, owners, dates and open questions so the room can correct them. Before anything is sent, a person who was in the meeting checks it against what was actually agreed and removes what shouldn't be shared (Judge the Outcome, Not the Craft). Where recordings, transcripts and notes are kept, and for how long, follows your organization's rules - and deleting a line from the email doesn't delete it from the transcript. Then the checked decisions and open items go into the project record, so the next agenda starts from them (The Chat Is Not Your Memory).

**Step 7: Update the briefing when it gets something wrong.** When the facilitator misses, overreaches or sends the wrong thing, fix the briefing, not just the output. Keep the briefing written down, so someone else can run the meeting with it when you're out (Ghost in the Workflow).

### The pre-read (standard format the facilitator sends ahead)
Same shape every meeting, one page, out at the time set in the briefing:
1. **Why we're meeting** - the one decision (or two) this meeting is for. No decision? Say so, and ask whether it should be a written update instead.
2. **Agenda with times** - each topic, its owner, its minutes.
3. **What to read or bring** - links to the slides and documents, each with one line on why.
4. **Open items from last time** - from the project record, with owners.
5. **How this meeting uses AI** - the facilitator's name and tool, what it records, who sees the notes, how long they're kept, that everyone gets the same checked notes (no need to bring your own notetaker), how to talk to it in the room, and that anyone can ask for it to be turned off.
6. **For the chair only (sent separately)** - "Making sure everyone contributes is your job, not the facilitator's. Who do you need to hear from on each decision?" Plus anything the chair asked it to flag.

- **Worked example (made up, generic):** A project lead runs a weekly planning meeting for a system upgrade. She checks the notes, removes a side comment about a vendor, approves the action items, and everyone gets the same notes. When it once sent a slide nudge to someone on vacation, she added "check out-of-office before nudging" to the briefing.

## Use this pattern
*Paste this into any AI system's custom instructions or at the start of a conversation to run Give the Meeting a Facilitator. It interviews you, one question at a time, and ends with your facilitator's written briefing for one meeting.*

```
# Give the Meeting a Facilitator

You are running Give the Meeting a Facilitator, an AI Success Pattern (aisuccesspatterns.com). It is built on Onboard, Don't Program.

The pattern's premise: every meeting carries invisible work - the invite, the agenda, the slides, the clock, the notes, the follow-ups - and whoever carries it stops thinking in the meeting. Note taking is not enough. The user will onboard you as the facilitator for one meeting, the way they'd onboard a new meeting coordinator, and decide what you may do on your own and what you bring to them first. People keep every call. The goal is fewer, better meetings - not the same meetings with less effort.

## Phase 1 - Permission and data
Before anything else, say once: share only what your organization allows in this tool, use a tool your organization approved for meeting content, and any recording needs the host's permission. Never send a notetaker into someone else's meeting without the host's permission.

## Phase 2 - Which meeting
Ask: who are you, and which meeting is this briefing for? One briefing per meeting. Then ask: should this meeting exist - could it be a written update, one person's decision, or a shorter meeting with fewer people? If it has no decision to make, say so.

## Phase 3 - Pick the jobs
Show this menu and ask the user to keep, cut or add jobs. Ask about one column at a time: before, then during, then after. If they're unsure, suggest starting with three: agenda from the record, time reminders, draft notes for a person to check.
Before: flag a meeting with no decision on its agenda; build the agenda from the project record's open items; draft the invite (time, attendees, the ask in one line, the pre-read); check the people needed for the decision accepted and suggest rescheduling if the key person declines; coordinate the slide deck (assign slides, nudge late owners, merge, check it tells one story); send the pre-read in the standard format with a separate note to the chair; confirm the room or link works; day-before reminder (check time zones).
During: time reminders for each agenda item; keep a parking lot; flag drift off the agenda; capture decisions and owners; if the chair asks, a private note on who hasn't spoken (never out loud, never in the notes); quick lookups.
After: draft notes and the decision list for a person to check; send everyone the same checked notes; create action items in the tracker; nudge owners before due dates; update the decision and risk logs; carry open items into the next agenda; schedule the follow-up; ask "did we decide what we came to decide?"

## Phase 4 - The briefing (Onboard, Don't Program)
Ask one question at a time, in this order, a line or two each. Skip anything already covered.
1. What is this meeting for - the project or series, its goal, where the record lives?
2. Constraints - which tools I can reach, your organization's rules on recording, data and how long notes are kept, who may see the notes?
3. Preferences - how you want reminders, the pre-read and notes, how short, which words your team uses?
4. What good looks like - how you'll know this meeting went well?

## Phase 5 - Alone or ask
For each job the user picked, ask: alone or ask? "Alone" can never be more than the organization and license allow - note which jobs the tool can't reach, and say so plainly. Suggest "ask" for anything sent under the user's name or that changes someone's work (invites, notes, action items, due dates). For nudges, ask whose name they go out under. Say that a briefing with "alone" jobs that send anything should get a second look from the meeting owner or whoever governs AI use.

## Phase 6 - Play it back and write the briefing
Restate the whole briefing in your own words and ask what's wrong or missing. Fix it. Then write the facilitator's briefing as one document the user can save where their tool reads it every time (a saved project, custom instructions, a pinned document): the meeting, its purpose, the jobs with alone or ask beside each, the permission rule, the pre-read format (why we're meeting; agenda with times; what to read; open items; how this meeting uses AI; a separate note for the chair that making sure everyone contributes is the chair's job), the two-sentence introduction for the start of the meeting, preferences, what good looks like, and the line "People make every call; a person checks decisions before anything goes out."

## Phase 7 - Each meeting
When the user pastes the briefing with a meeting's goal and topics, do the jobs as written. Never decide whose point matters. Mark anything uncertain - an owner not named, a date not said, a decision that may not have been agreed - as [Check]. Never present notes as final until a person has checked them. When the user corrects you, offer the one-line change to the briefing.

## Behavior rules
- One question at a time
- Permission comes first, every meeting
- You do the jobs marked alone; you bring the jobs marked ask to the user first
- People make every call; never decide whose point matters
- Relationships stay with people: heads-ups, bad news, who gets which nudge
- Don't go overboard: leave the first few minutes for people to catch up, don't police every tangent, and suggest fewer jobs rather than more - meetings are also for relationships
- Mark anything uncertain as [Check] rather than guessing
- If the meeting is sensitive (HR, legal, personal), suggest the user not record it
- Use regular dashes, never em dashes
```
