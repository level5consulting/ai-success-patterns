## The team

## The voices

**Gloria Fenwick (adversarial):**
"I'll say it plainly: in my business, time saved is a measure. If my two counter people stop working Saturdays because AI writes the order confirmations, I stop paying overtime. That's a number on my P&L, not smoke. The draft says cost savings are 'catching smoke with your hands' - that's true for a 500-person company with finance people building cash flow models. I don't have finance people. I have me.
Second, the calls-per-week math. If my inside rep saved 10 hours, the draft says she should take 20 more calls. Who's calling? In January nobody calls a landscaping supplier. You'd be grading her on demand she doesn't control. The draft does say 'look upstream' for the quote team - but it opens with 'they should be taking 20 more calls,' and that's the line a manager will remember.
Third, your own example measures time. '5 a week vs 1, 20 weeks vs 4' - that's time saved. A reader who's paying attention will say the pattern breaks its own rule in the example. If the real measure is patterns shipped and meetings booked, say that's the measure.
And the opening - an Anthropic employee's post and 'that is not possible.' You don't know his week. It reads like picking a fight with a stranger, and it dates the page. The point stands without him.
Last: 'teach them to fish' is nice, but I'd want to know what I do Monday. Which one measure do I pick first?"

**Renee Calloway (who carries this?):**
"I read 'an output measure for every team' and thought: who has the 'before' number? Calls per week before the AI rollout - nobody wrote that down. I'd be the one digging through the phone system to rebuild it. The draft should say you need a baseline, and that if you don't have one, you start counting now.
Step 3 - 'find 10 hours of organizational value for that role' - is great, but 'it is on us' doesn't say who. It's the manager's job, not the coordinator's and not the employee's. Say the manager.
What comes off my plate is real: the quarterly 'how many hours did AI save you?' survey I send out and then total. If this pattern lands, I stop sending it. Say that - people will love you for it.
The Start-here pages have the 'Who owns the work / What you stop doing' lines. This one needs them: who owns the output measure, who owns the backlog, and what stops - the hours survey.
And the backlog itself - where does it live? If there's no list of valuable work waiting, the manager can't 'find 10 hours' on the spot. One line on keeping a ready list would help."

**Dr. Priya Raman (analytics):**
"Moving from hours to output is the right move - hours saved is self-reported and nobody can check it. But output measures move for lots of reasons. Quotes per week can double because of a price change, a new rep or the season. If you compare to last month you'll credit AI for spring. Compare to the same period last year, or to a team that isn't using it yet. One sentence on that would make the measure believable to finance.
Second, the arithmetic. '10 hours saved, 30-minute calls, 20 more calls' assumes every saved minute converts to output and that capacity was the only limit. Usually it isn't. I'd present that as the question to ask - 'where did the 10 hours go?' - not the expected answer.
Third, pair every output measure with a guard measure. Greg already sees it with the content team - four times the content may make the site harder to use. More calls could mean shorter, worse calls. Calls per week plus customer satisfaction; quotes per week plus win rate.
Fourth, the example: 'a meeting that would not have happened yet.' That's a counterfactual. It's believable because Greg can point to dates on his results page - say how you know, so readers learn to ask the same of their own claims.
And the 60-hour claim - whether or not it's possible, the problem is that it measures input. Make that the point rather than 'that is not possible.'"

**Tom Becker (sales ops):**
"If finance stops accepting hours and starts accepting revenue and time to market, every team will walk in claiming revenue. That's my world - I've been praised for a quarter the market gave me. The bars in step 4 are the easiest ones to claim credit for. The draft needs a line on tying a claim to something that wouldn't have happened otherwise - Greg's meeting example does exactly that, so point at it as the test.
Second, pipeline isn't revenue. 'Those meetings in my sales funnel are worth money' - true, but a meeting isn't worth the whole deal. Finance will discount it by how often meetings turn into deals. If the page puts a deal value on a first meeting, the finance reader stops trusting the rest.
Third, the people side. If a rep reports 'AI saved me 10 hours' and the answer is 'great, take 20 more calls,' reps stop reporting savings. The draft should say how to ask so people keep telling you - reward the output, don't punish the honesty.
And keep the boat anchor. That's the line people will repeat in meetings. I'd want it near the top - it's the best picture of why unclaimed time costs money."
