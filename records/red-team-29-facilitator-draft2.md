**The work (confirmed with Greg at 4 PM, November 12):** test draft 2 (patterns/29-give-the-meeting-a-facilitator.md) - a string with Onboard, Don't Program; a menu of before / during / after responsibilities; what the facilitator may do alone vs. what needs a person; the briefing handed over at the start of each meeting. ## Rachel Kim - program manager

I like that this is finally about the whole meeting and not the transcript. The menu table is the most useful thing on the page - I could circle four boxes in a minute.

Where I get stuck is "one sitting." The prompt has six phases, and Phase 2 walks me through every column before I've said who I am. For me that's backwards: I don't know which jobs I want until I've said what meetings I run. I run a weekly status, a monthly steering committee and ad hoc escalations, and they need different jobs. The page assumes one facilitator for one meeting series. Tell me plainly: one briefing per recurring meeting, or one briefing for me with a section per meeting type.

Step 5 says "before each meeting, give the facilitator its standing briefing plus this meeting's goal." For a recurring meeting, that's the thing I'll stop doing by week three. Say how to make the standing part stick - saved as a project, custom instructions, a pinned document - in tool-neutral words, so I only add the one-line goal.

The time-zone reality: "a reminder the day before" means three different times for my team. Small, but it's the kind of thing the playback should catch, and the page could use it as the example of a gap the playback finds.

Last, I want a "start here" line: if you only pick three jobs, pick these. The draft says "most people start with three or four" but doesn't say which. I'd pick agenda from the record, time reminders, and draft notes for me to check.

## Tony Alvarez - Microsoft 365 admin

Governance is the right home, and "alone or ask" is the most governable idea on the page. I can write a policy around two words. But the page talks about what the facilitator may do as if the user decides it. Half of it isn't the user's call. Whether an AI can send invites from your calendar, write to Planner or Jira, or read the project files is set by IT and by the license. Step 3 should say that "alone" can never be more than your organization allows, and that the user's job is to check what the tool can reach before marking anything alone.

Retention is missing. Every recording, transcript and AI note is a record somebody may have to produce later. The page says who "sees" the notes - it should also say where they're kept and for how long follows your organization's rules, and that deleting a side comment from the email doesn't delete it from the transcript. That's the gap that bites people.

The tool-neutral line in When to use it is good. The one-line Teams mention is fine. But "any AI can work from ... a transcript recorded with permission" quietly invites people to paste a recorded meeting into whatever personal AI they use. Add "a tool your organization has approved for meeting content" - Avoid the Naughty List belongs in the string here.

The briefing is now an asset that grants permissions. Who reviews it? If a coordinator copies the project lead's briefing, they inherit her "alone" list. Ghost in the Workflow is in Step 7 for the right reason; I'd add that a briefing with "alone" jobs that send anything gets a second look from the meeting owner or whoever governs AI use.

## Denise Holloway - executive assistant

The menu is close to my real job, which tells me it's right. A few things I do every week that aren't there: confirming the room or the link actually works; knowing who must be there for the decision to count and checking they accepted - the "right people" box is about inviting, not about the decision-maker actually coming; rescheduling when the key person declines, which is half of my week; and handling the pre-meeting with the boss - the five minutes where I tell her who's upset and what she's walking into. That last one should stay with a person, and the page should say so.

"Tell the chair who hasn't spoken yet" - I'd keep it, but it should go to the chair privately, never out loud in the room. A tool announcing that Kevin hasn't spoken will embarrass Kevin and the chair won't use it twice.

What stays with people is too short. Add: the relationships. Who gets a heads-up before a decision lands on them, who should hear bad news in person instead of in the notes, which nudge goes to a VP and which never does. A facilitator that nudges my CEO's peers for slides is a facilitator I'd switch off. That's the "ask" column, but the page should name it as a people job, not just a setting.

Also: the after column says "nudge owners before their due dates." Who is the nudge from? If it comes from the AI it's ignorable; if it comes from the leader's account without her seeing it, it's a problem. The alone/ask step should give this as an example.

## Victor Lindqvist - organizational psychologist (adversarial)

I don't think this page solves the right problem, and I want to finish that argument.

The Problem section is honest: the meeting took an hour, the work around it took three. The draft's answer is to automate the three hours. My research answer is that most of those meetings shouldn't happen. A status meeting that needs eight people's slides merged at 11 PM is a meeting that should be a written update. When you make the invisible work free, you remove the friction that used to make people ask "do we need this meeting?" Expect more meetings, longer decks and more attendees, because the cost now lands on nobody. That's the opposite of what a Governance pattern should do.

So the first job on the menu - before the agenda - should be the question: should this meeting exist? Can it be a written update, a decision by one person, a shorter meeting with fewer people? If the facilitator is good at anything, it should be good at telling the leader "this meeting has no decision on its agenda." Right now the pattern only asks "did we decide what we came to decide?" at the end. Ask it at the start.

Second, the Value section measures prep hours saved and decisions per meeting. It doesn't measure total meeting hours, which is the number that matters for the people in the room. If prep hours fall and meeting hours rise, the pattern has failed, and the page would call it a success.

Third, "tell the chair who hasn't spoken" is surveillance dressed as inclusion. Counting airtime is not the same as knowing who has something to say. Silence is often a choice, and people who know they're being counted start talking to be counted. If you keep it, put the judgment explicitly with the chair and say the count is never recorded in the notes.

Finally, the leader at the center of this page is someone who runs a lot of meetings. Every efficiency you give her makes it easier to run more. The pattern needs a line that the goal is fewer, better meetings - not the same meetings with less effort.
