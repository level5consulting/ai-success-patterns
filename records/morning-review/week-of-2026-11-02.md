## Rant 4 - AI isn't new (draft 2, ready to post)
`2026-11-03-rant4` - night of Tuesday, November 3 - answered Tue Nov 3, 9:36 AM CT

When will people stop treating AI like it was invented last Tuesday?

I read a post this week that said nothing invented before AI could cut resources, increase output and improve quality all at once.

Nothing? I started my career writing COBOL. The whole job was cutting resources, increasing output and improving quality, all at once. That's what automation is. That's what it always was.

The example in the post: when a trial user logs in, AI looks at what they've built and sends them a personal message offering help. That's a trigger, a data lookup and a message. I built integrations like that on middleware platforms that don't even exist anymore. I'd tell you their names, but you'd have to be my age to recognize them, and I wouldn't wish that on anyone.

Here's what is new, and it's a big deal: who gets to build it. That used to take a programmer, a project and a budget request. Now a sales leader can do it in an afternoon.

That's the real story. AI didn't invent automation. It handed automation to a whole new group of people. And those people need what programmers learned the hard way: test it, document it, measure it, and don't let it send a customer something you wouldn't send yourself.

That's not living in the past. It's bringing back the past to help our future.

--- RANT 6, DRAFT 1 ---

I've been taking the beginner AI courses. Claude's. Microsoft's. Every one of them starts in the same place: email.

Draft your emails. Summarize your emails. Answer your emails faster.

Wrong place to start.

Email isn't overhead. For most of us it's the main way coworkers, customers and partners experience us. It's where trust gets built. And now my AI writes three polished paragraphs, your AI summarizes them into one line, and writes back three more. Two machines talking, two humans pretending communication got more efficient.

The courses also teach a prompting checklist. Context, goal, source, expectations. I lasted about three seconds into real work before I forgot it. Nobody remembers a checklist. People remember a name they can say out loud: "Did you run Avoid AI Slop?"

Here's why it matters. Early lessons become habits. If your first win with AI is email, email is what you'll keep using it for. You'll automate the easiest, most visible part of your job and leave the valuable part untouched.

The better rule: automate what nobody sees. The preparation, not the conversation. The research, the organizing, the red-team review before you hit send. This post is an example. AI challenged my thinking and found the holes. The opinions, and the typos, are still mine.

Stop giving your relationships to AI. Automate the invisible instead.

Bringing back the past to help our future.

## The Chat Is Not Your Memory - Ask for the agenda
`2026-11-05-agenda` - night of Thursday, November 5 - answered Fri Nov 6, 8:26 AM CT

--- NEW STEP 6 ---

Ask for the agenda. At any point in a session, ask the AI to create a meeting agenda for where the work stands. It forces a summary of what's decided and what's open, and it turns talk into decisions and next steps. It also shows drift cheaply: if the agenda is wrong, the AI has lost the thread, and you found out before the work did. Make it the closing move of every task chat: do the job, ask for the agenda, update the record with it, and use it to open the next chat.

--- IN THE PROMPT ---

When the work reaches a natural stopping point, or the user asks, offer a meeting agenda for where the work stands: what's decided, what's open, what's next. If the user says the agenda is wrong, you have lost the thread - go back to the record.

(Phase 4 now produces the agenda first, and it opens the next chat.)

--- IN PMO-START-HERE ---

Each chat reads the project record, does the job, asks for the agenda (what's decided, what's open, what's next), and updates the record with it. That agenda opens the next chat.
