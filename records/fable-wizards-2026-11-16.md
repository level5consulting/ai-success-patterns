## 1. The Wizard Who Asked First (Ten People, One Idea) - reworked - about 145 words

The queen of a small river kingdom asked her nine wizards how to stop the spring floods.

The night before, eight of them asked the Mirror of Answers. Eight scrolls arrived sealed in gold, and every one recommended "a harmonized enchantment of proactive flood resilience."

The ninth, young Wren, didn't start with the Mirror. She climbed to the hut of old Maud, who had watched that river for sixty springs, and asked her questions until the candle burned out. Where does the water rise first? What changed the year it got worse? Who saw it happen?

Then Wren wrote it all down and asked the Mirror to help her say it plainly.

Her scroll was the only one that mentioned the beaver dam on the east fork.

The queen read nine scrolls. She remembered one.

And that's why we say: Ten People, One Idea.

## 2. The Endless Scroll (The Chat Is Not Your Memory) - moved from the galaxy - about 160 words

Archmage Theo spent three months choosing a new supplier of dragon-fire for his tower - all of it in one endless conversation with his enchanted scroll. It never forgot a thing, and never complained. It just grew longer.

In the third month, the Council of Elders asked why he had ruled out the cheapest supplier.

Theo asked the scroll. It answered at once, in fine gold ink, with a clear and confident reason. Nobody had ever said it.

He unrolled the scroll down the tower stairs and out into the courtyard. The real reason was in there somewhere, between a supper order and a note to the guild's lawyers. He never found it.

He told the Council, "I'll send an owl shortly," which is what archmages say when they don't know.

Now Theo keeps one page: what we decided, why, and who said so. The scroll reads it every morning.

And that's why we say: The Chat Is Not Your Memory.

## 3. The Friendly Crystal (Assemble the Team) - kept as is (Greg: Keep) - 135 words

The night before she pitched the Merchants' Guild, a young witch asked her crystal ball what it thought of her plan.

"Brilliant," it said. She asked about her prices. "Wise." She asked about her rivals. "None worth worrying about." She went to bed happy.

The next morning, the Guild master turned to her fourth page and asked, "Whose customers are you taking?"

She didn't have an answer. The rest of the hour was polite.

On the road home she held up the crystal and said, "Be the merchant who said no this morning. Then be the farmer who'd buy from me. Then be the rival across the bridge."

The crystal clouded over and spoke in three voices. None of them were friendly. Her next pitch went better.

And that's why we say: Assemble the Team.
