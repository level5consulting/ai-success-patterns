## Draft
**Lead** [Synthesized]: Design the look in the AI first. Then have it build a small program that gets the numbers from the live data - the same way every time.

**What goes wrong:** People just mail it in - they get something that looks pretty and they use it. I was building a grading sheet for a portfolio. The projects had the basic data on schedule, cost and resources, and the grades came from weighted industry standards. The standards were easy to keep consistent. The problem: the AI aggregated the data differently every time the scorecard ran. The data hadn't changed, but the portfolio's grade moved between A, B, C, D and F.

**What to do:**
1. Design the look and feel first - in the AI. It's faster than anything I've used; you can even feed it pictures of something you drew on paper. The design has to be right, because it tells the AI what data it needs to fill in the visual.
2. Then have the AI build a small program that gathers the data and fills in the visual - the same way every time.
3. Test it before you trust it. You can't assume the first pass is correct. I calculated the numbers by hand, fed them into a spreadsheet and built the formulas; next time I'd have the AI build that spreadsheet too. This is Test Adversarially - and learning a little testing theory isn't hard.

**Where the idea comes from:** An AI programmer once told me: once you have the look and feel you want, tell the AI to program it so it runs faster. This is the same thing, only for accuracy instead of speed - and you get the speed for free. People will say this is 90s programming theory, and it is. Our new class of programmers - the AI chataholics - just don't know these ways of working.

**If you don't program:** this will be your first time using The Compressed Apprenticeship. Also find a real mentor who does program - you should only need a small amount of their time.

**When to use it** [Synthesized]: any visual or report you'll run more than once on data that changes - a scorecard, a status report, a dashboard.
