## The team

All four are fictional. One is adversarial.

- **Marcy Delgado** - PMO director at a regional health system, 40 projects, deciding whether to try the patterns with her team.
- **Rick Hanlon** - owns a four-person travel agency; uses ChatGPT for itineraries and client emails.
- **Joan Ferris (adversarial)** - CFO of a mid-size manufacturer; has thrown out every vendor ROI slide she's been handed for ten years.
- **Sam Okafor** - independent PMO consultant who also buys training for clients; read the patterns and then the Services page (Quick Start $2,500, small-business version $750; Half-Day Session $4,000 onsite, $3,500 virtual; 6-Month Retainer $40,000).

## Picks and comments

**Marcy - B.** "I'd screenshot the box and put it in my steering deck, and the blank row is the part I'd actually fill in. A sentence buried in a list won't survive a copy-paste into PowerPoint. I trust it because it names BLS and says pay only. I'd ignore it the moment it showed a total, because nobody in my organization books five reviewers for one business case."

**Rick - A.** "A $100,000 table makes me feel like the page isn't for me; I've never hired a financial analyst in my life. One line I can read and move past, and 'insert your own numbers' is fair. What would make me trust it is an example my size, like two hours of my own time on a client proposal."

**Joan (adversarial) - A, reluctantly.** "Both are a salary next to a tool, and the gap between 'covers the work' and 'saves a salary' is where I stop reading. A at least doesn't put dollars in a column where my eye adds them up; B is one sum away from a '$286,620 in savings' claim someone will make on LinkedIn. I'd trust either only if it says plainly that nobody's pay went away, and that the saving is hours, which I can check."

**Sam - A.** "Every pattern page has Services and Talk to Greg in the navigation. Put B's table there and the reader does the math in one click: $2,500 to cover $102,320 of work. That's a price comparison Greg hasn't decided to make, and it cheapens the free pattern into a sales sheet. A reads as information. B reads as a pitch, and I'd discount everything else on the page because of it."

## The team's pick: Option A (3 to 1)

The deciding reason: B's table of salaries sits one click from Greg's prices, so it reads as a price comparison and invites readers to add the column into a savings total, which is exactly the claim the pattern says it isn't making. A says the same thing as information. Marcy's point stands, though: the reader-fills-it-in idea is strongest in B, and the changes below carry it into A.

## Changes the team would make to A (each is Greg's call)

1. Put "Insert your own savings numbers here" first in the line, before any dollar figure, so the reader meets their own numbers before ours. (Marcy, Joan)
2. Add hours as the thing to measure: "count the hours you'd spend booking and running those reviews, and use your own pay rates." (Joan)
3. Add one plain sentence that no one's pay goes away: "This is time back, not a cut." (Joan; matches the rule that a pattern never replaces a person)
4. Keep A to two example roles, never three, so there is nothing to add into a total. (Joan, Sam)
5. Keep any dollar figures off the Services page and the ROI sheets until Greg decides to make that comparison on purpose. (Sam; already the note in `records/hires-you-dont-need.md`)
6. Offer a fill-in version for decks: a downloadable one-page sheet (or a starter-pack worksheet) with the blank "your numbers" row from B, for PMO leads who want it in a steering deck. (Marcy)
7. Add a small-business example to the line where it fits, in hours, not salary, e.g. "or a few hours of your own time on a client proposal." (Rick; no BLS figure needed)
