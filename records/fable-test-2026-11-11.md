## The fable - Ten People, One Idea (148 words)

In the valley town of Mendle, everyone wrote their own letters. The baker's smelled of rye and ran too long. The miller's were three words and a drawing. The schoolteacher argued with herself in the margins.

One spring a peddler sold the town a clever quill that wrote beautifully on its own. Soon every letter in Mendle was graceful, tidy and kind, and the post rider couldn't tell who wrote what.

That autumn the miller saw a crack under the old bridge. So did the baker, and so did the schoolteacher. All three wrote to the council. The council read three graceful letters, each saying politely that repairs might be considered, and filed them together.

The bridge fell in the first flood.

Now, in Mendle, everyone writes the first line by hand before the quill may touch the page.

And that's why we say: Ten People, One Idea.

*How it maps to the pattern:* the quill is the shared AI; every letter got better and they all sounded the same, so three different people seeing the same danger read as one polite idea. The town's fix is the pattern's first step - capture each person's own view before AI touches it - and the quill still writes the rest.

## Talk idea - "Three Fables and an Eye Chart" (one paragraph)

A 20-minute talk told as three short fables, each about two minutes and each ending with a pattern name the room can say out loud. First, the town of Mendle and its clever quill - Ten People, One Idea. Second, a lighthouse keeper who trusted his memory of the lamp schedule instead of the logbook - The Chat Is Not Your Memory. Third, a king whose advisors all agreed with him until he seated a stranger at the table whose job was to disagree - Assemble the Team. Between fables, a few minutes on what each pattern looks like at work on Monday morning. The close is the eye chart: a slide of generic AI tips shrinking line by line into a blur nobody can read, and above them, in letters you can read from the back of the room, the five Start-here names - Avoid AI Slop with an LLM Interview, The Chat Is Not Your Memory, The Night Shift, Assemble the Team, Ghost in the Workflow. People forget tips. They remember stories, and names they can say out loud.

**The team** (run unattended - Greg is asleep - so the team is up for his OK in the form):

3. **Rick Dorsey** - program chair for a professional association chapter who fills a monthly speaker slot. He tests whether the talk fits a real program and a real audience.

**Joan Pruitt:** "It's a nice little story. My question is whether I'd book it. My leaders want to walk out with something to do on Monday. A talk that's mostly fables sounds like the soft session before lunch. If each fable comes with one concrete thing people do at work - a step, a sentence they can say in a meeting - then the story is the hook and the step is the value. Without that, I'd pass. Also, a collapsing bridge is a big swing for a point about writing that sounds alike. Make sure the room doesn't hear 'AI will get people killed.'"

**Maya Lindqvist:** "Read aloud, it works - short sentences, three named townsfolk, a clear turn, a clean ending. 'Argued with herself in the margins' is the best line; keep it. Two things. The moral says ten people, but the story has three letter writers - a careful listener will notice. Either have ten letters reach the council or let the gap stand on purpose. And make sure the ending doesn't read as 'throw away the quill.' The town still uses it - say so, in one short line, or the fable sounds anti-AI."

**Rick Dorsey:** "Chapter meetings usually want 45 to 60 minutes, and our members want something they can use. A 20-minute version works for a meetup, but I'd need a longer version with a short exercise - have people write their own first line before the AI, like Mendle. And the eye chart close is strong, but give them a card to take home with the five names on it, or the top line is gone by the parking lot."

**Changes raised** (no recommendations - Greg decides each one):
1. Pair each fable with one concrete thing people do at work on Monday (Joan Pruitt)
2. Soften or swap the bridge collapse so the fable doesn't sound like "AI will get people killed" (Joan Pruitt)
3. Fix the number mismatch - have ten letters reach the council, or keep three on purpose (Maya Lindqvist)
4. Add one short line showing the town still uses the quill, so the fable isn't anti-AI (Maya Lindqvist)
5. Build a 40-60 minute version with a short "write your first line" exercise for chapter meetings and Minnebar (Rick Dorsey)
6. Hand out a take-home card with the five Start-here names at the close (Rick Dorsey)
