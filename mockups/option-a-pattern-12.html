Pattern 12 · Governance

Ghost in the Workflow

The person who built your AI workflow understood it. They knew why the prompt was worded that way, what the agent was never supposed to touch, and which outputs needed a second look. Then they moved on, and the workflow kept running without them.

The problem

The builder did everything right - and it all lived in their head

Picture the builder who does it well. Before building anything, they wrote down what success looked like (Pattern 01, Begin with the End in Mind). They gave the AI rich context about the business, the audience, and the constraints instead of a list of commands (Pattern 02, Onboard Don't Program). They tried to break it before trusting it, and tuned it around the failures they found (Pattern 03, Test Adversarially). Where numbers mattered, they had the model write code to calculate rather than calculate itself (Pattern 10, Models Are Not Good at Math). The agent works because of all that discipline.

Now ask where that discipline lives. The value statement is in a chat thread. The onboarding context is spread across forty prompt revisions. The red-team findings - the edge cases, the inputs that broke it, the step where a human must check the output - exist only in the builder's memory. So does the answer to a question nobody thought to ask: what systems and data the agent can reach, and why it needs that access.

The value

AI assets that outlast the people who built them

Cut the hidden cost of turnover. Gallup estimates replacing an employee costs one-half to two times their annual salary, and part of that cost is the time it takes a replacement to become productive. For a $120,000 role, the total is $60,000 to $240,000. When the departing person's AI work documents itself, the successor inherits working knowledge instead of reverse-engineering it, which shortens that ramp.
Catch silent drift before it costs you. Documented assumptions let a successor see which data, policies, and dependencies an agent relies on, and check them when they change. Measure it: share of AI assets with documented dependencies, and how long it takes to spot a broken one.
Inherit an asset, not a liability. The successor can change, improve, or retire the workflow with confidence, instead of choosing between blind trust and starting over.
Keep access defensible. Every AI asset has a named owner who can explain what it reaches and why, so permissions can be reviewed rather than left in place. Measure it: share of AI assets with a named owner and a current access review.
When it applies

When this pattern applies

This pattern applies from the moment anyone other than the builder depends on an AI asset. That includes agents running on a schedule or triggered by events, prompts a team reuses, chat workflows built into how a process gets done, and any AI that touches business systems or data. The test is simple: if the builder disappeared tomorrow, would someone need to understand this?

How to do it

Make documentation part of the AI's job

The fix is the thing that makes this problem different from every undocumented spreadsheet before it: the AI can document its own work. Instead of asking the builder to write it all down afterward, make documentation part of the AI's job description from the start. The AI produces the work, and it produces the record that explains the work.

  1. Write documentation into the job descriptionWhen you onboard the AI (Pattern 02), add a standing constraint: every output that others will rely on comes with a record a stranger could follow. For example: Standing constraintAs you build and refine this workflow with me, maintain a running record for whoever inherits it. Capture what it does and why, the decisions we made and the alternatives we rejected, the assumptions it depends on, the systems and data it can access and why, the failures we found in testing, and the points where a human must check the output. Update the record whenever we change something. Write it for someone who wasn't in this conversation.
  2. Scale the record to the riskNot every AI asset needs the same record. Match the depth to how much damage a misunderstanding could do. A lite record is enough for reusable prompts and chat workflows that a person runs by hand. It covers four things: what it's for, the key assumptions it depends on, where a human must check the output, and who owns it.
  3. Keep the record with the work, not in the chatA record inside a chat history is still a ghost. Store it alongside the agent, prompt, or workflow it describes, wherever the team already keeps shared work, and version it with every change. If someone can find the agent, they can find its record. If you build in a chat tool, make it a habit: at the end of each working session, have the AI output the updated record as a file, and save it next to the workflow.
  4. Test the record with someone who didn't build itHand the record to a colleague who wasn't involved and ask them three questions. What does this do? What would you check if the main data source changed? Is it safe to change, and how would you know? If they can't answer, the record isn't done. This is Pattern 03 applied to the documentation itself.
  5. Review the record when the world changesSet triggers, not just dates: the owner changes role, a data source or system is restructured, a policy the workflow depends on changes, or the output starts to look off. Each trigger means opening the record, checking its assumptions, and updating it. That's how silent drift gets caught. Sometimes the answer is that the workflow is no longer needed - and the record's retirement section says what to revoke and what will break when it goes.
